Every authorised medicine in the European Union comes with **product information**: the summary of product characteristics for health professionals, the package leaflet for patients, and the text on the packaging. It is written by the company that markets the medicine and approved by regulators.

For most of its history, that information has been designed for paper. Even online, it is usually a PDF: a picture of a printed page.

## From document to data

**Electronic product information (ePI)** is the same approved content, published in a structured digital format. Instead of one long document, it becomes a set of clearly labelled sections that software can recognise: what the medicine is for, how to take it, possible side effects, and so on.

In the EU, regulators have agreed a common standard for ePI. It is based on **HL7 FHIR** (Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources), an international standard for exchanging health information that is widely used by health IT systems.

## Why it matters to patients

Structured information opens possibilities that a PDF cannot:

- **Always up to date.** When the approved text changes, the digital version can be updated straight away, rather than waiting for new printed leaflets to reach pharmacies.
- **Easier to read.** Text can be resized, read aloud, or shown in a layout that works on a phone.
- **Easier to navigate.** People can jump to the section they need, and tools can bring relevant sections into focus.
- **Connected.** With a person's permission, ePI can be linked with other health information, for example to highlight a warning that applies to them.

## What ePI does not do by itself

A standard is a foundation, not a finished service. Someone still has to write information clearly, design tools that people find helpful, and check that those tools really improve understanding.
